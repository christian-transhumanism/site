Christians are often told they must choose: faith or reason, revelation or
inquiry, the church or the modern world.

Pope Benedict XVI did not think so. He thought the choice was a category
error — and that the Enlightenment, of all things, was the proof.

His case is more interesting, and more demanding, than the one-line version
that circulates online. It is not a compliment paid to modernity, and it is not
a complaint against it. It is an argument about where reason comes from, what
happens when it is cut off from its source, and why the people best placed to
defend it may be the ones modernity assumed it had left behind.

### An unlikely witness

On April 1, 2005, Cardinal Joseph Ratzinger accepted the St. Benedict Award
at the Convent of St. Scholastica in Subiaco. John Paul II died the following
day. Eighteen days later, Ratzinger was Pope Benedict XVI.

In that address he made a claim that still has not been absorbed:

> "In this connection, the Enlightenment is of Christian origin and it is no
> accident that it was born precisely and exclusively in the realm of the
> Christian faith."

This is not a concession from a friend of modernity. Ratzinger spent
twenty-four years as the Vatican's chief guardian of doctrine, and was among
the sharpest critics of relativism the last century produced. His argument is
not that Christianity should make peace with reason. It is that Christianity
has always been the religion of reason, and forgot — and that the modern world,
having taken up reason in its place, is now in danger of forgetting too.

The famous line is the hinge of a four-part argument. It is worth following
each step, because the conclusion only lands if the steps hold.

### Step one: modern reason has cut off its roots

Ratzinger begins with a diagnosis of the reason modern culture actually uses.

He is not against that culture. He grants that it has produced genuine and
universal goods: religious liberty, the equal dignity of every person, the
separation of powers, the rights of the individual against the state. He does
not want any of it undone.

His objection is to the account of reason underneath it. The dominant modern
view is, broadly, positivist: only what can be measured and tested counts as
knowledge. Questions of meaning, purpose, and the good fall outside reason's
jurisdiction and become matters of private preference. Ratzinger's charge is
that this is not reason's full scope but a slice of it — a powerful slice,
excellent at telling us *how*, and silent about *why* and *whether*. It is,
he says, not man's complete reason but only a part of it.

A reason that narrow cannot account for its own foundations. It cannot say why
persons have dignity, why freedom is worth having, or why truth is worth
seeking. Those convictions came from somewhere — from a moral and religious
memory that once gave reason its direction. Positivism lives on that memory
while denying it any standing. Ratzinger's image for the result is simple:
*a tree without roots dries up.*

Two things follow. First, a freedom that will not say what freedom is *for*
eventually turns on itself, hardening into a new dogmatism that tolerates less
and less dissent. Second, relativism, which presents itself as humility,
becomes its own kind of absolutism: it claims to be the final stage of reason,
and treats every tradition that asks larger questions as a relic waiting to be
outgrown.

So the problem with modern reason, on Ratzinger's account, is not that it is
too rational. It is that it is not rational *enough*. It has forgotten where
its own commitments came from.

### Step two: those roots are Christian

If the roots have been cut, what were they? Here is where the argument turns —
and where a reader expecting a standard conservative lament gets surprised.

> "From the beginning, Christianity has understood itself as the religion of
> the 'Logos,' as the religion according to reason. In the first place, it has
> not identified its precursors in the other religions, but in that
> philosophical enlightenment which has cleared the path of traditions to turn
> to the search of the truth and towards the good."

This is a striking claim about Christian self-understanding. When the early
church looked for its ancestors outside Israel, it did not find them in the
temples of the pagan gods. It found them in the philosophers — those who had
left inherited myth behind to reason their way toward the one God. The Gospel
of John begins by naming Christ the *Logos*: the Word, the reason, the rational
principle through which all things were made. The faith announced itself, from
its first page, as a claim about the rational structure of reality.

Christianity, in other words, did not merely tolerate philosophy's break with
tradition. It identified with it. The faith sided with the people who asked
whether the gods were true over the people who asked only whether the rites
were correct.

Ratzinger then names three things that follow from this, each of which the
Enlightenment would later claim as its own:

1. **Freedom of faith.** Christianity began as the religion of the persecuted
   and spread as a universal faith across peoples and empires. It therefore
   denied that the state had any right to own religion as a branch of civic
   order — which implies that faith must be free.
2. **Universal human dignity.** Christianity defined every human being, without
   distinction, as a creature and image of God, and so as holding the same
   dignity in principle.
3. **Reason as faith's native language.** Because the faith confessed the
   Logos, the search for rationality — including the rationality of faith
   itself — was always its own prerogative.

Liberty of conscience, the equality of persons, and the authority of reason:
take those three away and there is not much Enlightenment left. Ratzinger's
point is that none of them is self-evident. Most civilizations in history have
not held them. They arrived carried by a specific story about God and humanity.

That is the sense in which the Enlightenment is "of Christian origin." Not that
every Enlightenment thinker was a believer — many were not — but that the
values they fought for were Christian values, and the confidence in reason they
wielded was a Christian confidence. The roots modern reason has cut are the
very ones it grew from.

### Step three: the church domesticated reason — and earned the correction

If the Enlightenment's values were Christian, why did the Enlightenment so
often have to fight the church to win them?

Ratzinger does not dodge this. It is the hardest sentence in the address, and
it is aimed at the church rather than at its critics. The Enlightenment arose,
he says, "whenever Christianity, against its nature and unfortunately, had
become tradition and religion of the state," because in that condition "the
voice of reason had been too domesticated."

Note the two qualifiers: *against its nature* and *unfortunately*. A faith
whose founding claim was that the state cannot own religion became the state's
religion. A faith that had chosen the philosophers over the temples became, in
its turn, a temple — an inherited order to be defended rather than a truth to
be sought. In doing so it contradicted itself.

And note the word: *domesticated*. Not rejected — kept. House-trained. Reason
was still present in Christendom, still useful, still fed, and no longer free
to go anywhere. When faith became a pillar of public order, the questions it
once welcomed became threats to that order. So reason went somewhere else.

On this account the Enlightenment is not an attack Christianity survived. It is
a correction Christianity earned:

> "It was and is the merit of the Enlightenment to have again proposed these
> original values of Christianity and of having given back to reason its own
> voice."

*Again proposed.* The Enlightenment did not invent these values; it handed
them back to the people who had let them lapse. *Was and is.* Present tense.
The correction is not finished, and the church still needs it. He then points
to the Second Vatican Council's *Gaudium et Spes* for underlining "this profound
correspondence between Christianity and the Enlightenment," and calls the
reconciliation of Church and modernity "the great heritage that both sides must
defend."

Both sides. This is the structural center of the whole argument, and it is
symmetrical. In one era, the Enlightenment corrected a Christianity that had
domesticated reason. In ours, Christianity must help correct an Enlightenment
that has amputated reason. Each tradition, at its best, holds the other to its
own deepest commitments. Neither is safe alone.

### Step four: the question that actually divides us

If faith versus reason is the wrong axis, what is the right one? Ratzinger is
specific:

> "…the problem is whether the world comes from the irrational, and reason is
> not other than a 'sub-product,' on occasion even harmful of its
> development — or whether the world comes from reason, and is, as a
> consequence, its criterion and goal."

That is the real fault line. Is reason fundamental to reality, or a late
accident thrown up by a blind process?

This is where steps one through three converge. A positivist reason that has
cut its roots has, implicitly, already answered the question: reason is a
by-product, a useful tool evolved by creatures in an indifferent universe. But
that answer creates problems it cannot solve.

If reason is a by-product of the irrational, its success is a puzzle. Why
should minds assembled for survival happen to grasp the mathematics of
galaxies? Why should the universe be intelligible at all — and intelligible in
the elegant, compressible language of physics? And if reason has no standing in
the nature of things, its conclusions about value have no standing either.
Dignity, freedom, and truth become conventions, and conventions can be revised
by whoever holds power. Step one's tree without roots is the practical result
of this theoretical answer.

Christianity's answer is that the world comes from Logos — that rationality is
not an anomaly in the universe but its origin and its aim. "Criterion and
goal": reason is both the standard reality is measured by and the end it is
moving toward. Which means the Christian claim about reason is the *more*
ambitious one. To reason is not to work against the grain of things. It is to
participate in what the universe is made of.

This is why Ratzinger says Christianity's philosophical strength today lies
exactly here. The faith is not reason's rival but its guarantor — the one
account on offer in which reason's trustworthiness is not a lucky accident.
He adds the distinctively Christian turn: the creative reason at the root of
the world is not cold calculation. It is the reason that reveals itself, on
the cross, as love.

### The argument in brief

1. Modern culture runs on a narrowed, positivist reason that cannot account for
   its own commitments to dignity, freedom, and truth. It has cut its roots.
2. Those roots are Christian. Christianity understood itself from the start as
   the religion of the Logos, sided with the philosophers against inherited
   myth, and taught free faith, universal dignity, and the authority of reason.
3. The Enlightenment had to recover those values because the church, having
   become a religion of state, betrayed its own nature and domesticated reason.
   The Enlightenment's correction was deserved — and still is.
4. The real divide is therefore not faith against reason but whether the world
   comes from reason or from the irrational. Only the first answer secures
   reason itself, and Christianity gives that answer.

There are fair objections. Historians will press on "exclusively": Greek
philosophy, the scholars of the Islamic Golden Age, and classical Chinese
thought all prized reason, and the European Enlightenment drew on more than one
stream. Ratzinger's claim is best read not as a denial of that, but as a
narrower one: that the particular synthesis — reason, universal dignity, and
free conscience bound together and turned into a civilizational program — took
shape inside Christendom and drew on Christian premises. Indeed, he names the
Greek philosophers as Christianity's own chosen ancestors. Secular readers may
also suspect that "the Enlightenment was really ours" is a convenient story for
a church that often resisted it. Step three is Ratzinger's answer: he concedes
the resistance outright. The church, in his telling, *needed* to be corrected.

### What this asks of us

The Christian Transhumanist Affirmation says we are "guided by Jesus' greatest
commands" — including to love God "with all your heart, soul, **mind**, and
strength" — and that science and technology are "tangible expressions of our
God-given impulse to explore and discover."

Benedict's Subiaco address is a reminder that this is not a novel position. It
is the tradition remembering itself.

It also explains why such a position is needed now. If Ratzinger is right, the
secular world inherited reason from Christianity and is struggling to keep it
rooted. Christians who abandon the frontiers of science and technology leave
that struggle to others. Christians who engage them bring something the moment
lacks: a reason to believe reason itself is trustworthy, and an account of why
discovery matters — because the universe is the work of a creative reason we
are invited to share.

And it carries a warning. When Christianity forgets that it is the religion of
the Logos, reason does not disappear. It leaves. Whatever we build now — in
science, in technology, in the pursuit of human flourishing — the question is
whether the church will be a place reason can live, or one more place it has to
escape.

> "We Christians must be very careful to remain faithful to this fundamental
> line: to live a faith that comes from the 'Logos,' from creative reason, and
> that, because of this, is also open to all that is truly rational."
>
> — Joseph Ratzinger, Subiaco, April 1, 2005
