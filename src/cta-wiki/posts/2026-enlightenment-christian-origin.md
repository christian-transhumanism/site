Christians are often told they must choose: faith or reason, revelation or
inquiry, the church or the modern world.

Pope Benedict XVI did not think so. He thought the choice was a category
error — and that the Enlightenment, of all things, was the proof.

His case is more interesting, and more demanding, than the one-line version
that circulates online. It is not a compliment paid to modernity, and it is not
a complaint against it. It is an argument about where reason comes from, what
happens when it is cut off from its source, and why the people best placed to
defend it may be the ones modernity assumed it had left behind.

### An unlikely witness

On April 1, 2005, Cardinal Joseph Ratzinger accepted the St. Benedict Award
at the Convent of St. Scholastica in Subiaco, the place where Benedict of
Nursia first withdrew to pray in the sixth century. John Paul II died the
following day. Eighteen days later, Ratzinger was Pope Benedict XVI.

The address he gave there, usually published as *Europe in the Crisis of
Cultures*, was written against the backdrop of the fight over the proposed
European Constitution — and specifically over whether its preamble should
mention God or Europe's Christian roots. (It did not.) Ratzinger read that
quarrel as the surface of something deeper: a question about whether a
civilization can keep the goods it inherited while denying where they came
from.

In the middle of that address he made a claim that still has not been absorbed:

> "In this connection, the Enlightenment is of Christian origin and it is no
> accident that it was born precisely and exclusively in the realm of the
> Christian faith."

This is not a concession from a friend of modernity. Ratzinger spent
twenty-four years as the Vatican's chief guardian of doctrine, and was among
the sharpest critics of relativism the last century produced. His argument is
not that Christianity should make peace with reason. It is that Christianity
has always been the religion of reason, and forgot — and that the modern world,
having taken up reason in its place, is now in danger of forgetting too.

To see why, it helps to follow the address in order, because the famous line
is the hinge of a longer argument, not a slogan standing on its own.

### Step one: power has outrun wisdom

Ratzinger does not open with philosophy. He opens with technology.

We live, he says, in a time of great dangers and great opportunities. Human
power has expanded enormously — over matter, over energy, and now over life
itself. But the capacity to *use* that power well has not kept pace:

> "Moral strength has not grown together with the development of science;
> rather, it has diminished."

His examples are the ones that still dominate the ethics of emerging
technology: weapons of mass destruction falling into the hands of terrorists,
and a biotechnology able to read and rewrite the human being. His sharpest
worry is that people may come to be treated as *products* — designed, selected,
and evaluated by someone else's specifications — rather than received as gifts.
The logic he fears is captured in a single line: *what one knows how to do, may
also be done.*

It is worth being precise about what he is and is not saying. He is not saying
that science is bad or that knowledge should stop. He is saying that capability
without a matching growth in moral reason turns power into a power of
destruction. And he names the reason that growth has stalled: a technical
mentality that treats measurable things as real and pushes moral claims into
the realm of private preference.

Anyone who has followed the debates over AI safety, gene editing, or autonomous
weapons will recognize the problem. Twenty years later, it reads less like a
sermon than a forecast.

### Step two: a reason that cut off its roots

Why has moral strength diminished? Ratzinger's diagnosis is that the dominant
culture of the modern West has adopted a *narrowed* form of reason.

He calls it a "radical Enlightenment culture." Its defining value is freedom,
and he grants that it has produced genuine and universal goods: religious
liberty, the equal dignity of every person, the separation of powers, the
rights of the individual against the state. He does not want any of that
undone.

But this culture rests on a positivist account of reason — the view that only
what can be empirically tested counts as knowledge, so that questions of
meaning, purpose, and the good are matters of opinion. Ratzinger's objection is
that this is not reason's full scope but a slice of it, and a culturally
specific slice at that. Cut off from the moral and religious memory of humanity
that once gave it direction, it has nothing left to tell power *no*. Morality
shrinks to a calculation of consequences, and the calculation can always be run
again.

His image for this is simple: *a tree without roots dries up.*

Two consequences follow. First, a freedom that will not say what freedom is
*for* eventually turns on itself — a "confused ideology of freedom," in his
phrase, hardening into a new dogmatism that tolerates less and less dissent.
Second, relativism, which presents itself as humility, becomes its own kind of
absolutism: it claims to be the final stage of reason, and treats every
religious tradition as a relic waiting to be outgrown.

This, he argues, is why the real cultural conflict of our time is not between
the world's religions. It is between a program of human emancipation from God
and the great religious cultures together. On the European Constitution, he
puts it pointedly: what troubles other faiths is not the mention of God in
public life, but "the attempt to build the human community absolutely without
God."

### Step three: the Enlightenment's roots are Christian

Here is where the argument turns — and where a reader expecting a standard
conservative lament gets surprised.

If the problem were simply "the Enlightenment versus Christianity," the answer
would be simple: retreat from the Enlightenment. Ratzinger refuses that answer,
because he thinks the premise is false. The Enlightenment is not an alien force
that attacked Christianity from outside. It grew from Christian soil.

> "From the beginning, Christianity has understood itself as the religion of
> the 'Logos,' as the religion according to reason. In the first place, it has
> not identified its precursors in the other religions, but in that
> philosophical enlightenment which has cleared the path of traditions to turn
> to the search of the truth and towards the good."

This is a striking claim about Christian self-understanding. When the early
church looked for its ancestors outside Israel, it did not find them in the
temples of the pagan gods. It found them in the philosophers — those who had
left inherited myth behind to reason their way toward the one God. The Gospel
of John begins by naming Christ the *Logos*: the Word, the reason, the rational
principle through which all things were made. The faith announced itself, from
its first page, as a claim about the rational structure of reality.

Ratzinger then names three things that follow from this, each of which the
Enlightenment would later claim as its own:

1. **Freedom of faith.** Christianity began as the religion of the persecuted
   and spread as a universal faith across peoples and empires. It therefore
   denied that the state had any right to own religion as a branch of civic
   order — which implies that faith must be free.
2. **Universal human dignity.** Christianity defined every human being, without
   distinction, as a creature and image of God, and so as holding the same
   dignity in principle.
3. **Reason as faith's native language.** Because the faith confessed the
   Logos, the search for rationality — including the rationality of faith
   itself — was always its own prerogative.

Liberty of conscience, the equality of persons, and the authority of reason:
take those three away and there is not much Enlightenment left. Ratzinger's
point is that none of them is self-evident. Most civilizations in history have
not held them. They arrived in Europe carried by a specific story about God and
humanity.

### Step four: why it had to happen anyway

Then comes the hard sentence — the one aimed at the church rather than at its
critics. The Enlightenment arose, Ratzinger says, "whenever Christianity,
against its nature and unfortunately, had become tradition and religion of the
state," because in that condition "the voice of reason had been too
domesticated."

Domesticated. Not rejected — kept. House-trained. Reason was still present in
Christendom, still useful, still fed, and no longer free to go anywhere. When
faith became a pillar of state power, the questions it once welcomed became
threats to order. So reason went somewhere else.

On this account the Enlightenment is not an attack Christianity survived. It is
a correction Christianity earned:

> "It was and is the merit of the Enlightenment to have again proposed these
> original values of Christianity and of having given back to reason its own
> voice."

*Was and is.* Present tense. The correction is not finished, and the church
still needs it. He then points to the Second Vatican Council's *Gaudium et Spes*
for underlining "this profound correspondence between Christianity and the
Enlightenment," and calls the reconciliation of Church and modernity "the great
heritage that both sides must defend."

Both sides. This is the structural center of the whole address. The
Enlightenment corrected a Christianity that had domesticated reason. Now
Christianity must help correct an Enlightenment that has amputated reason.
Each tradition, at its best, holds the other to its own deepest commitments.
Neither is safe alone.

### Step five: the question that actually divides us

If faith versus reason is the wrong axis, what is the right one? Ratzinger is
specific:

> "…the problem is whether the world comes from the irrational, and reason is
> not other than a 'sub-product,' on occasion even harmful of its
> development — or whether the world comes from reason, and is, as a
> consequence, its criterion and goal."

That is the real fault line. Is reason fundamental to reality, or a late
accident thrown up by a blind process?

Notice what this does to the usual debate. If reason is a by-product of an
irrational universe, then the success of reason is a puzzle. Why should minds
assembled for survival happen to grasp the mathematics of galaxies? Why should
the universe be intelligible at all? And if reason has no standing in the
nature of things, its moral conclusions have no standing either — which brings
us straight back to step one, and power with nothing to answer to.

Christianity's answer is that the world comes from Logos — that rationality is
not an anomaly in the universe but its origin and its aim. Which means the
Christian claim about reason is the *more* ambitious one. To reason is not to
work against the grain of things. It is to participate in what the universe is
made of. Ratzinger adds the distinctively Christian turn: the creative reason
at the root of the world is not cold calculation. It is the reason that
reveals itself, on the cross, as love.

### Step six: reversing the axiom

The address ends with a proposal aimed at nonbelievers as much as believers.

Early modern thinkers, beginning with the jurist Hugo Grotius, tried to ground
morality in a way that would hold *etsi Deus non daretur* — "even if God did not
exist." The idea was reasonable for its time: after a century of religious war,
Europe needed a moral foundation that confessional rivals could share. And for
a while it seemed to work, because the moral certainties of the Christian
inheritance were still in the air everyone breathed.

Ratzinger's argument is that this strategy has run its course. Once the
inherited certainties wear thin, the "as if God did not exist" foundation turns
out to have been borrowing all along. So he proposes reversing the axiom. Even
those who cannot find their way to belief, he suggests, should try to live
*veluti si Deus daretur* — "as if God existed" — an invitation he traces to
Pascal. Not as coercion, and not as pretense, but as a recognition that human
dignity, freedom, and moral seriousness are most secure in a world understood
to come from reason and love.

And then he closes where he stands — at Subiaco. What the moment needs, he
says, is not new programs but people: men and women whose faith is enlightened
and lived, who keep their gaze on God and so make God credible to others. His
model is Benedict of Nursia, who withdrew into a cave during the collapse of
Rome and came out to found Monte Cassino and a monastic tradition that would
preserve learning, cultivate land, and help build a new civilization out of
the ruins of an old one.

### What to make of it

Read as a whole, the argument runs like this:

1. Human power is growing faster than moral wisdom.
2. That is because modern culture has narrowed reason to the measurable, cutting
   it off from the moral and religious roots that gave it direction.
3. But the Enlightenment's own best goods — free conscience, equal dignity, the
   authority of reason — came from those roots, specifically from Christianity's
   self-understanding as the religion of the Logos.
4. The Enlightenment happened because Christianity, entangled with state power,
   had betrayed that self-understanding. It was a needed correction.
5. Now the correction must run the other way, and the decisive question is
   whether the universe comes from reason or from the irrational.
6. A civilization that wants to keep its inheritance should live as if reason,
   and the God who is reason and love, are real.

There are fair objections. Historians will press on "exclusively": Greek
philosophy, the scholars of the Islamic Golden Age, and classical Chinese
thought all prized reason, and the European Enlightenment drew on more than one
stream. Ratzinger's claim is best read not as a denial of that, but as a
narrower one: that the particular synthesis — reason, universal dignity, and
free conscience bound together and turned into a civilizational program — took
shape inside Christendom and drew on Christian premises. Secular readers may
also suspect that "the Enlightenment was really ours" is a convenient story for
a church that often resisted it. Ratzinger anticipates that suspicion by
conceding the resistance outright. The church, in his telling, *needed* to be
corrected.

What is harder to dismiss is the diagnosis. The gap between what we can do and
what we know we should do is now the central fact of technological life. A
culture that cannot say what freedom is for, or why persons have dignity, will
struggle to govern technologies that can redesign persons.

### What this asks of us

Christian transhumanists will read this address with particular interest,
because it touches both of our commitments at once.

Ratzinger's warnings about biotechnology are sometimes cited as if they were a
case against human enhancement as such. That misreads his argument. His target
is not technology but technology without moral reason — power that obeys
*what can be done* rather than *what ought to be done*. That is exactly the
problem Christian transhumanism exists to address. We hold that science and
technology are part of the mission of God, and that Christ offers an ethical
vision for scientific and technological progress. The answer to power outrunning
wisdom is not less power. It is more wisdom, and a community that takes both
seriously.

The Christian Transhumanist Affirmation says we are "guided by Jesus' greatest
commands" — including to love God "with all your heart, soul, **mind**, and
strength" — and that science and technology are "tangible expressions of our
God-given impulse to explore and discover." Benedict's Subiaco address is a
reminder that this is not a novel position. It is the tradition remembering
itself.

It also explains why such a position is needed now. If Ratzinger is right, the
secular world inherited reason from Christianity and is struggling to keep it
rooted. Christians who abandon the frontier of science and technology leave
that struggle to others. Christians who engage it bring something the moment
lacks: a reason to believe reason itself is trustworthy, and a vision of the
person that makes enhancement answerable to love.

And it carries a warning. When Christianity forgets that it is the religion of
the Logos, reason does not disappear. It leaves. Whatever we build now — in
science, in technology, in the pursuit of human flourishing — the question is
whether the church will be a place reason can live, or one more place it has to
escape.

Benedict of Nursia did not save civilization by opposing the tools of his age.
He built communities where prayer, learning, and work belonged together, and
the future grew from them. That is still the task.

> "We Christians must be very careful to remain faithful to this fundamental
> line: to live a faith that comes from the 'Logos,' from creative reason, and
> that, because of this, is also open to all that is truly rational."
>
> — Joseph Ratzinger, Subiaco, April 1, 2005
